The Fox and the Fallen Grapes

After Aesop. Plate by Milo Winter, from The Aesop for Children, 1919.

A fox came upon a vine of grapes hanging high above her head. She leapt once, twice, a third time, and each time her jaws closed on nothing but air. At last she walked away with her nose in the air, announcing to the empty road, “No matter. They were sour anyway — hard and green and fit only for wasps.”

She had gone barely twenty paces when a gust of wind came down the hill, shook the vine, and sent the whole cluster tumbling into the dust behind her.

The fox stopped. She looked back. Then she turned and ran, and fell upon the grapes, and ate them down to the last one, stem and skin and seed, licking the juice from her whiskers when she was done.

A crow who had watched the whole business from a branch called down, “Sour, were they?”

“Sweet,” said the fox, without looking up. “Wonderfully sweet.”

“But you said—”

“I said what I needed to say when I could not have them,” said the fox. “Now I can have them. A creature who cannot change her opinion as fast as her luck changes will go hungry twice.”

Moral: Some people despise only what they cannot reach — and the moment it falls within reach, the contempt falls away with it.
