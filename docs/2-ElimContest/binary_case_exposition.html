Elimination Contests: The Binary Case

Four players, two rounds, private information — and one sharp tension: winning early reveals your strength, which hurts you later. We derive the equilibrium that resolves it, from scratch, term by term.

Setting two-round all-pay tournament Types two (High / Low) Read ~15 min

0 The tension, in words

Imagine a small tournament. Four contestants are split into two pairs. Each pair fights; the winner of each advances; the two winners meet in a final. Every fight is decided by effort — whoever tries harder wins — but effort is costly and spent whether you win or lose.

Here is the twist that makes the problem interesting. After the first round, everyone sees how hard each contestant fought. A contestant who blew the field away in round one has just announced, “I value winning a lot” — and that reputation follows them into the final, where a frightened-looking opponent will fight them harder. So a strong contestant faces a dilemma: fight hard enough to advance, but not so hard that you reveal how strong you are.

The rest of this document turns that sentence into equations and solves them. We will find that both strong and weak types deliberately blur together — they choose overlapping levels of effort so that a given effort level does not pin down who you are. That blurring is the equilibrium, and it has a surprisingly clean closed form.

1 The model, precisely

Everything below is built from a handful of primitives. We define each one before it is used.

Definition — All-pay auction

A contest in which every participant pays their own bid, win or lose, and the highest bid wins the prize. The “bid” is effort; the payment is that effort’s cost. This is the standard model of a contest where effort is sunk.

Definition — Type and value

Each player has a private type, their value for winning. In the binary case there are two types: High with value $v_H$ and Low with value $v_L$, where $v_H > v_L > 0$. Each player is High or Low with probability $\tfrac12$ each, drawn independently (my type tells you nothing about yours — independent private values, IPV).

The game has two rounds:

  • Round 1. Within each pair, the two players play an all-pay auction. The winner advances. All four round-1 bids are then made public.
  • Round 2. The two group-winners play an all-pay final. Now each knows only the distribution of the other’s type, updated by the public round-1 bids.

A player’s type stays private throughout — the only channel through which information leaks is the bid. That is what makes round-one effort a signal.

Definition — Strategy and the bid CDF $G$

A (symmetric) strategy tells each type how to randomize its round-one bid. Collect the resulting spread of bids across the whole population into one cumulative distribution function $G(b)$ = probability that a random player bids at most $b$. Because winning a pair means beating one rival drawn from $G$, $G(b)$ is also your probability of winning round one with bid $b$.

Definition — Belief (reputation) $\mu$

After seeing a round-one bid $b$, an observer forms the posterior $\mu(b) = \Pr(\text{type is High}\mid \text{bid}=b)$. This number — between 0 and 1 — is the finalist’s reputation. A bid that only High types would make gives $\mu = 1$; a bid only Low types make gives $\mu = 0$.

Definition — Co-mixing (pooling)

Both types randomize over a shared, overlapping range of bids. Because their supports overlap, a single observed bid could have come from either type, so it does not fully reveal the type — $\mu(b)$ lands strictly between 0 and 1. This is the opposite of a separating equilibrium, where each type picks a distinct range and every bid gives its owner away.

The classical result (Zhang & Wang, 2009) is that no symmetric separating equilibrium survives here: full revelation is self-defeating, exactly because of the tension in §0. That leaves the co-mixing equilibrium — which is what we characterize.

2 What “solving” means

We look for a Bayes–Nash equilibrium: a pair (strategy, beliefs) that is internally consistent in two directions.

  1. Beliefs come from the strategy. Given how each type bids, $\mu(b)$ is fixed by Bayes’ rule.

  2. The strategy best-responds to the beliefs. Given the reputation each bid will earn, no type wants to deviate — every bid a type uses must yield the same expected payoff (else it would shift mass to the better bid).

The unknowns are therefore the bid distribution $G$ and the belief map $\mu$. The engine that ties them together is the round-two payoff — how much a finalist expects to earn as a function of the two reputations at the table. So we compute that first.

3 The final round, in closed form

Two facts make the final tractable.

3.1 — Who you actually face

Your final opponent is not a random player: it is the winner of the other pair, i.e. the higher of two independent draws from $G$. The CDF of the maximum of two independent draws is the square of the CDF.

Fact — the opponent’s bid law is $G^2$

Let $F$ be the CDF of the round-one bid of the finalist you meet. Then $F = G^2$.

This is the quiet workhorse of the whole analysis: it makes a finalist’s reputation-weighted payoff an explicit function of $G$, dissolving what would otherwise be a circular round-1 ↔ round-2 fixed point.

3.2 — Payoffs at the table

Consider a finalist with reputation $\mu$ (their own) facing an opponent with reputation $\nu$. Solving the two-type all-pay final and reading off the equilibrium utilities gives, with $\Delta := v_H - v_L$:

$$\pi_2(\mathrm{H};\mu,\nu) = \Delta\cdot\bigl(1 - \min(\mu,\nu)\bigr), \qquad \pi_2(\mathrm{L};\mu,\nu) = \frac{v_L\,\Delta}{v_H}\cdot(\mu - \nu)^{+}$$

where $(x)^+ = \max(x, 0)$. Intuition: a High finalist loses value only when the opponent is at least as fearsome (the $\min$); a Low finalist earns a sliver only when it out-reputes the opponent (the $(\cdot)^+$).

3.3 — Expected value of reaching the final

Average those payoffs over the opponent’s reputation — whose law is fixed by $F = G^2$ — to get the continuation value: what a finalist of each type expects to earn, as a function of their own reputation $\mu$.

Definition — auxiliary $Y$, and constant $K$

Write $Y(\mu) = \int_0^{\mu} G^2$ — the reputation-average that the opponent’s $G^2$ law produces (so $dY/d\mu = G^2$) — and the constant $K = v_L\Delta / v_H$.

Continuation values
$$\tilde W_H(\mu) = \Delta\cdot(1 - \mu + Y) \qquad\qquad \tilde W_L(\mu) = K\cdot Y$$

Read $\tilde W$ as “the prize you expect from reaching the final, given the reputation you show up with.” Note the sign of the signal: a higher own-reputation $\mu$ lowers $\tilde W_H$ (the $-\mu$ term) — being seen as strong hurts you. That is §0 made algebraic.

4 The bottom of the bidding

Before solving the interior, we pin down where the support starts. Two short all-pay arguments settle it.

  1. The support starts at zero. If the lowest active bid were some $b_{lo} > 0$, a bidder there could shave it toward 0 without losing any probability of winning round one (nobody bids in the gap below), so they strictly save cost. Hence $b_{lo} = 0$.

  2. The reputation at the bottom is zero. At the very bottom $Y = 0$, so $\tilde W_L = K\cdot Y = 0$. A Low type is willing to sit at the bottom only if it earns nothing there — which is consistent only with belief $\mu_0 = 0$. So the belief starts at 0 and rises continuously: no jump, no gap.

Consequence. There is a mass point (an “atom”) of bidders exactly at $b = 0$, and it is carried by Low types alone. Since belief rises with the bid, no High type wants to hide down there.

5 Indifference becomes a differential equation

On the range where a type mixes, every bid it uses must give the same expected payoff. Differentiating that flat-payoff condition is what produces the governing equations.

Definition — interim payoff

A type-$v$ player bidding $b$ wins round one with probability $G(b)$ and then collects the continuation value $\tilde W$ at the reputation that bid earns, paying $b$ regardless. So the interim payoff is $G(b)\cdot\tilde W(v, \mu(b)) - b$.

Setting the derivative of the interim payoff to zero (it is constant across the support) gives the indifference condition, one per active type:

$$G'\cdot\tilde W(v, \mu) + G\cdot\tilde W_{\mu}(v, \mu)\cdot\mu' = 1$$

When both types mix, we have two such equations — linear in the two unknown slopes $g = G'$ and $G\mu'$. Solving the 2×2 system by Cramer’s rule gives the pair that drives everything. Write primes on $\tilde W$ for $\partial/\partial\mu$, and set $D = \tilde W_L\,\tilde W_H' - \tilde W_H\,\tilde W_L'$.

The governing system (†)
$$g = \frac{\tilde W_H' - \tilde W_L'}{D} \qquad\qquad G\cdot\mu' = \frac{\tilde W_L - \tilde W_H}{D}$$

Substituting the continuation values from §3.3 makes the right-hand sides explicit in $G$ and $\mu$: $\tilde W_H' = \Delta(-1 + G^2)$ and $\tilde W_L' = K\cdot G^2$.

To integrate it, use $\mu$ as the clock (it runs cleanly from 0 to its top) with state $(G, Y, b)$, starting at $(G_0, 0, 0)$:

$$\frac{dG}{d\mu} = G\cdot\frac{\tilde W_H' - \tilde W_L'}{\tilde W_L - \tilde W_H}, \qquad \frac{dY}{d\mu} = G^2, \qquad \frac{db}{d\mu} = \frac{G\cdot D}{\tilde W_L - \tilde W_H}$$

You could stop here and just integrate numerically. But this system hides a conserved quantity — and finding it collapses the whole thing to a formula.

6 The conserved quantity, and a closed form

Here is the load-bearing step of the entire project.

First integral
$$\Pi = G\cdot\Bigl[(1 - \mu) + \frac{\Delta}{v_H}\,Y\Bigr] = G_0 \quad\text{(constant along the whole co-mixing range)}$$

Differentiating $\Pi$ along the (†) flow, the $\Delta/v_H$ coefficient is exactly the one that makes every term cancel, so $\Pi' = 0$. Evaluating at the bottom (where $\mu = 0$, $Y = 0$, $G = G_0$) fixes the constant at $G_0$. Here $G_0$ is the size of the atom at zero — the one number left to determine.

Now solve $\Pi = G_0$ for the belief. Two gifts fall out:

  1. $Y$ becomes algebra — no integral needed:

    $$Y(G) = \frac{v_H}{\Delta}\cdot\Bigl(\frac{G_0}{G} - (1 - \mu)\Bigr)$$

  2. The belief becomes an explicit formula. Substituting back reduces (†) to a single separable ODE in $G$; the quadrature $\int dG / (1 - s^2 G^2)$ produces an inverse hyperbolic tangent. With $s = \sqrt{\Delta / v_H}$:

    $$\mu(G) = 1 - \frac{G_0}{G} + G_0\cdot s\cdot\bigl[\operatorname{arctanh}(sG) - \operatorname{arctanh}(sG_0)\bigr]$$
What is arctanh? The inverse of the hyperbolic tangent, $\operatorname{arctanh}(x) = \tfrac12 \ln \frac{1 + x}{1 - x}$. It appears here purely as the antiderivative of $1/(1 - s^2 G^2)$ — no hyperbolic geometry is being invoked, just that integral.

So the belief along the mixing range is known in closed form once we know the single number $G_0$. The bid follows too: since a High type is indifferent, its interim payoff equals a constant $U_H$, so $b = G\cdot\tilde W_H - U_H$. Evaluating at the bottom gives $U_H = \Delta\cdot G_0$, and a few lines of algebra using the first integral collapse it to a strikingly simple form:

$$b(G) = v_L\cdot\bigl[G_0 - G\cdot(1 - \mu(G))\bigr]$$

7 Closing the model: one number, one equation

Only $G_0$ remains. It is pinned by consistency with the prior: averaging the belief over all bids must return the population share of High types, which is $\tfrac12$. In the coordinate $G$ this reads:

$$\int_{G_0}^{1} \mu(G)\,dG = \frac12$$

One equation, one unknown. For the canonical values $v_H = 3,\ v_L = 1$ it solves to:

The (3, 1) solution — two regions

$G_0 = 0.2221$  ·  atom mass $2G_0 = 0.4443$  ·  top belief $\mu_{\max} = 0.9525$  ·  top bid $b_{\mathrm{top}} = 0.1746$  ·  $U_H = 0.4443$, $U_L = 0$.

The support has exactly two pieces: a Low-only atom at 0, then a co-mixing interval along which the reputation climbs from 0 up to $\mu_{\max} < 1$ — high, but never certain. Nobody is ever revealed for sure.

8 A wall: where certainty first becomes reachable

Can the reputation ever reach $\mu = 1$ — a bid that only High types make? Setting $\mu = 1$ in the first integral forces $G = G_0 v_H / (\Delta\cdot Y(1))$. For a moderate gap the right side exceeds 1 — impossible, since $G \le 1$. So $\mu = 1$ is simply unreachable, and the co-mixing interval is all there is.

As the gap $v_H/v_L$ widens, that inequality slackens, and at a precise threshold it reverses:

The no-tail wall

Certainty first becomes reachable at $v_H/v_L = 4.2286$. Below it: two regions, top reputation strictly below 1. Above it: a genuine $\mu = 1$ region opens up — and the closure of §7 no longer suffices, because the top of the support detaches from $G = 1$.

9 Wide gaps: the cap, and what it is not

Past the wall, the belief hits 1 at some interior point $G^* < 1$. Above it, the reputation is pinned at certainty — so it is no longer a mixing region. Only High types bid there. This is a new, third region.

9.1 — The cap’s bid, from a one-line argument

On the cap only High bids, so there is nothing to average over: the reputation term freezes, $Y$ is stuck at its entry value $Y_1 = (v_H/\Delta)(G_0/G^*)$, and $\tilde W_H = \Delta\cdot Y_1$ is constant. Single-type indifference ($G\cdot\tilde W_H - b = U_H$) integrates immediately to an affine bid:

$$b(G) = G\cdot\Delta\cdot Y_1 - \Delta\cdot G_0, \qquad \text{from } b(G^*) = v_L G_0 \text{ up to } b_{\mathrm{hi}} = G_0\cdot\Bigl(\frac{v_H}{G^*} - \Delta\Bigr)$$

The bottom of the cap meets the top of the co-mix exactly at $v_L G_0$ — the pieces join continuously. The kink there is nothing mysterious: it is the switch from the 2×2 Cramer system to the single-type relation. The two unknowns $(G_0, G^*)$ are pinned by $\mu(G^*) = 1$ together with the tail-inclusive closure $\int_{G_0}^{G^*} \mu\,dG + (1 - G^*) = \tfrac12$.

9.2 — The decisive question: is this a “reveal tail”?

One might fear the cap is the leading edge of full revelation — High types sprinting toward bids near $v_H$ and giving themselves away. It is not, and the numbers say so at every gap.

What the wide-gap equilibrium is

The equilibrium persists past the wall, gaining a High-only $\mu = 1$ cap. Two things are true of it at every ratio:

It is never a reveal tail. $b_{\mathrm{hi}}/v_H$ decreases from 0.054 toward 0.0334 — the cap is always a small fraction of the top prize and never approaches it.

It is a low-effort cap. The whole cap sits below $v_L$ for $4.2286 < v_H/v_L < 26.689$; beyond that it edges modestly above $v_L$, but this is the same equilibrium — same equations, all incentives intact — not a new regime. And the reputation rises monotonically to exactly 1: there is no hump, no non-monotonicity.

10 How we know it is right

The closed form and the raw system are two representations of the same dynamics, so they must agree — and checking that they do is the proof that the formula is correct where we had no independent anchor.

  1. Two independent solvers agree. Integrating the raw (†) equations directly (never touching the arctanh formula) reproduces $G_0$, $G^*$ and $b_{\mathrm{hi}}$ from the closed form to one part in $10^8$, at every ratio out to 200.

  2. It recovers the known case. At $(3, 1)$ it returns the §7 numbers to all printed digits.

  3. The incentives hold. Bids and beliefs are monotone; the belief reaches exactly 1; the High type is indifferent across the whole support to one part in $10^{14}$; and a Low type strictly prefers not to enter the cap.

The upshot: a contest where revealing strength is costly does not collapse into full revelation, nor into a shapeless mess. It settles into a crisp, computable shape — an atom of the timid, a band where the two types deliberately blur together, and, when the stakes are lopsided enough, a quiet cap of the strong bidding just a little more than the weak — but never enough to give themselves away.
