An economist's reading of shadow monotonicity

Quota games, Owen's multilinear extension, and pivotality premia — a companion to Docs/proofs.tex §sec:SM and §sec:odrop. Nothing here is new mathematics: every statement is one already banked, restated in the language of contests and power indices, with the derivations written out in full. The point is that the whole (SM) layer, which reads as opaque probability algebra, is a sequence of statements about who competes with whom for the last prize.

Every identity below was checked over the exact rationals — Docs/data/econ_reading_ids.jl, 23 664 checks, 0 failures, boards $3\le m\le 8$, all levels $k$, Julia 1.12.6, no floating point anywhere.

1. The contest is a quota game

There are $m$ contestants and $k$ prizes. Fix a reference level $c$ — a score, a grade, a threshold. Each contestant $\ell$ draws independently and lands

below $c$ with probability $x_\ell = G_\ell(c)\in(0,1)$ — $\ell$ does not outrank the reference;
above $c$ with probability $1-x_\ell$ — $\ell$ does outrank it.

So $x_\ell$ is contestant $\ell$'s own CDF value at $c$: high $x_\ell$ means $\ell$ is weak — likely to fall short — and $1-x_\ell$ is $\ell$'s threat.

Now put contestant $i$ at the reference level and ask for the probability that $i$ takes a prize. Contestant $i$ finishes in the top $k$ exactly when at most $k-1$ rivals outrank him:

$$W_i(x) \;=\; \Pr\big[\,\#\{\ell\ne i:\ \ell \text{ above}\}\le k-1\,\big].$$

Two features of this object drive everything that follows, and both are economic, not technical.

First, $W_i$ does not contain $x_i$. Contestant $i$'s own quality has been used up in placing him at $c$; what is left to determine is only what the others do. This is why the diagonal of every derivative matrix below is zero, and why one player can be added to the board without changing his own prospects.

Second, $W_i$ is increasing in every $x_j$, $j\ne i$: a weaker rival is good news. So the $m$-vector $W(x)=(W_1(x),\dots,W_m(x))$ is a monotone summary of the field, and the project's headline question — is $W$ injective on $(0,1)^m$? — asks whether a contest's outcome statistics recover its contestants' quantiles.

2. Owen's multilinear extension, in two lines

Let $v:2^{N}\to\{0,1\}$ be a simple game on $N$ — $v(S)=1$ if coalition $S$ wins. Owen (1972) defined its multilinear extension by letting each player join independently, $i$ with probability $x_i$:

$$f(x)\;=\;\sum_{S\subseteq N}\Big(\prod_{i\in S}x_i\prod_{i\notin S}(1-x_i)\Big)\,v(S) \;=\;\mathbb E\big[v(\tilde S)\big]\;=\;\Pr[\tilde S\text{ wins}].$$

The only structural fact we need is visible from the sum: $f$ is affine in each coordinate separately (each $x_i$ appears to the first power). Splitting the sum according to whether $i\in S$,

$$f(x)\;=\;x_i\,f(x\mid x_i{=}1)\;+\;(1-x_i)\,f(x\mid x_i{=}0). \tag{2.1}$$

Specialise to the symmetric quota game on $d$ players with quota $q$ — $v(S)=1$ iff $|S|\ge q$. Then $f(z)=\Pr[\#\{\text{joiners}\}\ge q]$, the Poisson–binomial upper tail.

Our contest is exactly this game, with the "coalition" being the set of contestants who fall short. On a board of $d$ contestants, write $A$ for the number above. Then

$$\Psi_k^{(d)}(z)\;:=\;\Pr[A\le k-1]\;=\;\Pr\big[\#\{\text{below}\}\ \ge\ d-k+1\big],$$

so $\Psi_k^{(d)}$ is Owen's multilinear extension of the quota game $(d;\,g+1)$, where

$$g \;:=\; d-k \qquad\text{— the number of non-prize slots, and the quota is }g+1.$$

In words: the prize is released iff at least $g+1$ of the $d$ fall short — equivalently, iff at most $k-1$ of them clear the bar. $g$ is the depth of the losing tail, and it is the invariant that organises the whole proof (§12).

3. One extension, evaluated $m$ times

Compare the two displays. On a board of $m$, contestant $i$'s rivals number $m-1$, and $i$ wins iff at most $k-1$ of them are above. That is the same functional, one board down:

$$W_i^{(k,m)}(x)\;=\;\Psi_k^{(m-1)}(x_{-i}),\qquad x_{-i}:=(x_\ell)_{\ell\ne i}.$$

The win-probability map is a single Owen extension evaluated at the $m$ leave-one-out profiles. Every contestant reports the field minus himself.

Worked example, $m=3$, $k=2$ (so $g=1$; a rival pair blocks you only if both clear the bar). With $x=(\tfrac12,\tfrac13,\tfrac14)$ the above-probabilities are $(\tfrac12,\tfrac23,\tfrac34)$ and

$$W_1=1-\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac34=\tfrac12,\qquad W_2=1-\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac34=\tfrac58,\qquad W_3=1-\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac23=\tfrac23,$$

while the full-board value is $\Psi_2^{(3)}(x)=\Pr[A\le1]=\tfrac1{24}+\tfrac14=\tfrac7{24}$. Hold on to these numbers; §7 explains why $\Psi$ is smaller than every one of the $W_i$.

4. Owen's derivative is a power index

Differentiate (2.1) — the coefficient of $x_i$ is what is left:

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x)\;=\;f(x\mid x_i{=}1)-f(x\mid x_i{=}0) \;=\;\Pr[\,i\text{ is pivotal}\,]. \tag{4.1}$$

This is Owen's identity, and it is the reason multilinear extensions belong to economics rather than to combinatorics: the gradient of $f$ is the vector of probabilistic power indices. Evaluate it at $x=(\tfrac12,\dots,\tfrac12)$ and you get the Banzhaf index; integrate it along the diagonal $x=t\mathbf 1$, $t\in[0,1]$, and you get the Shapley–Shubik index. Both classical indices are readings of the same derivative at different participation profiles.

For our quota game, $j$ is pivotal exactly when the last prize hangs on $j$'s own draw — when exactly $k-1$ of the others are above, so that $j$ going above pushes the count to $k$ and closes the prize:

$$p_j\;:=\;\frac{\partial \Psi_k^{(d)}}{\partial z_j}(z)\;=\;\Pr\big[A_{z_{-j}}=k-1\big]\;>\;0 . \tag{4.2}$$

Economically $p_j$ is $j$'s pivotality: the probability that the contest is on a knife edge at $j$. It is strictly positive at every interior profile, which is the analytic reason $\Psi_k$ is strictly increasing — every contestant always has some power.

5. The Jacobian is a matrix of rivalries

Chain (4.2) through §3. For $j\ne i$,

$$M_{ij}\;:=\;\frac{\partial W_i}{\partial x_j}(x) \;=\;\Pr\big[A_{x_{-\{i,j\}}}=k-1\big],\qquad M_{ii}=0 . \tag{5.1}$$

Read the right-hand side: it is the probability that, with $i$ and $j$ both set aside, exactly $k-1$ of the remaining field is above — that is, the probability that $i$ and $j$ are competing for the last prize. So $M$ is a matrix of head-to-head rivalry intensities. Three consequences are immediate and each is a familiar structural fact wearing economic clothes:

  • $M_{ij}=M_{ji}$: rivalry is mutual. Hence $M$ is symmetric and $W$ is a gradient field.
  • $M_{ii}=0$: you are never your own rival. This is the zero diagonal.
  • $M_{ij}>0$: everyone competes with everyone, at every interior profile.

The potential is worth naming. Let $A$ be the number of contestants above the bar on the full board and put

$$F(x)\;:=\;\mathbb E\big[(k-A)_+\big]\qquad\text{— the expected number of unfilled prize slots.}$$

$F$ is multilinear, so by (2.1) its derivative is a difference; and since $(a+1)_+-(a)_+=1\{a\ge0\}$ for integer $a$, writing $A_{-i}$ for the above-count excluding $i$,

$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_i} =\mathbb E\big[(k-A_{-i})_+-(k-1-A_{-i})_+\big] =\Pr[A_{-i}\le k-1]=W_i .$$

$W=\nabla F$ and $M=\nabla^2F$ with $F=\mathbb E[(k-A)_+]$: a contestant's win probability is the marginal effect of his own weakness on the expected slack in the prize schedule, and rivalry is the cross-effect. The whole model is the Hessian of one scalar — the expected shortfall of the quota.

One further fact, imported rather than derived: $M$ has inertia $(1,m-1,0)$ at every interior profile — one positive direction and $m-1$ negative ones (lem:signature, from Murai–Nagaoka–Yazawa's strict log-concavity theorem). The positive direction is the common one: everybody getting weaker together raises everybody's odds. Every other perturbation of the field is a reallocation, and reallocations trade off. That is the sense in which the contest has exactly one aggregate and $m-1$ compositional dimensions.

6. The shadow: who is $\Psi$ on the full board?

$W_i$ evaluates $\Psi_k$ at the $m-1$ rivals of $i$. What object is $\Psi_k^{(m)}$ evaluated at all $m$?

Add a hypothetical contestant $0$ at the same reference level $c$, making a board of $m+1$. His win probability is $\Pr[\text{at most }k-1\text{ of the }m\text{ incumbents are above}]$, which is $\Psi_k^{(m)}(x)$ — and, by the first structural fact of §1, it does not depend on his own type at all.

$\Psi_k^{(m)}(x)$ is the win probability of an entrant facing the field $x$: a contestant who counts for the others' arithmetic but whose own quality is invisible to his own payoff. That is the shadow. "Shadow monotonicity" is a statement relating what happens to the $m$ insiders to what happens to this one outsider.

7. The pivotality premium — the one identity to remember

Incumbents and the entrant face the same field, but the incumbent gets to omit himself. Exactly how much is that worth? Write $A_{-j}$ for the above-count over the field minus $j$. The event "$j$ wins" is $\{A_{-j}\le k-1\}$; the event "the entrant wins" is $\{A\le k-1\}$ with $A=A_{-j}+1\{j\text{ above}\}$. So the first event contains the second, and the difference is precisely

$$\{A_{-j}\le k-1\}\setminus\{A\le k-1\}\;=\;\{A_{-j}=k-1\}\cap\{j\text{ above}\},$$

because losing $j$'s own contribution can only matter at the knife edge. Taking probabilities and using independence and (4.2):

$$\boxed{\;W_j(x)\;-\;\Psi_k^{(m)}(x)\;=\;\underbrace{(1-x_j)}_{j\text{'s threat}}\cdot \underbrace{p_j}_{j\text{'s pivotality}}\;\ge\;0\;} \tag{7.1}$$

The incumbent's edge over the entrant is his own threat probability times his own pivotality: the value of not having to compete against yourself, which bites exactly when you would have been the marginal rival.

This is lem:odropid(e) in proofs.tex — the "cap-gap identity" — and it is the single most useful sentence in the whole layer. Three corollaries fall out at once.

(a) The entrant is the worst-placed player in the room: $\Psi_k^{(m)}(x)\le\min_j W_j(x)$. Check it on §3's numbers: $\tfrac7{24}\approx0.29$ against $\min(\tfrac12,\tfrac58,\tfrac23)=\tfrac12$. The premia are $\tfrac5{24},\tfrac13,\tfrac38$, and indeed $\tfrac12-\tfrac7{24}=\tfrac5{24} =\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac5{12}$.

(b) Equality holds iff $x_j=1$. A contestant who is certain to fall short has zero threat, hence zero premium: he is the entrant. Keep this — it is the hinge of §14.

(c) A sum rule. Rewrite one premium as a joint probability, $(1-x_j)\,\Pr[A_{-j}=k-1]=\Pr[\,j\text{ above},\,A=k\,]$, and add over $j$. The sum counts the above-contestants on the event $\{A=k\}$, which is $k$ of them:

$$\sum_{j=1}^m W_j(x)\;=\;m\,\Psi_k^{(m)}(x)\;+\;k\,\Pr[A=k]. \tag{7.2}$$

On §3's numbers: $\tfrac12+\tfrac58+\tfrac23=\tfrac{43}{24}$ and $3\cdot\tfrac7{24}+2\cdot\tfrac{11}{24}=\tfrac{43}{24}$. In words: the average incumbent beats the entrant by $(k/m)\Pr[\text{the prizes are exactly filled}]$. All the aggregate advantage of incumbency lives on the knife-edge event where the field exactly exhausts the prize schedule.

8. Shadow monotonicity, stated in economics

Here is def:SM verbatim: at $(k,m)$, if $W(z)<W(y)$ coordinatewise then $\Psi_k(z)<\Psi_k(y)$. Translated:

If every incumbent's prize probability is strictly lower in field $z$ than in field $y$, then an entrant's is too.

Contrapositive: if an outsider does at least as well against $z$ as against $y$, then at least one insider does too. The outsider is never the odd one out.

It is a no-composition-paradox statement, and it is not obvious, for a reason that is easy to state and hard to dismiss: the hypothesis is $m$ leave-one-out comparisons and the conclusion is a leave-none-out comparison. Each incumbent grades the field with himself excised, and no two incumbents excise the same player. Whether $m$ such comparisons aggregate to a statement about the whole field is exactly a Simpson's-paradox question.

The naive argument is unavailable, and it is worth seeing why. One would like to say: $W$ is increasing, so $W(z)<W(y)$ gives $z<y$, and $\Psi_k$ is increasing, so we are done. The first step is false — indeed it is equivalent to the injectivity theorem the whole project is trying to prove. Concretely, at $m=3$, $k=2$, take

$$z=\big(\tfrac35,\tfrac3{10},\tfrac3{10}\big),\qquad y=\big(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12\big).$$

Then $W(z)=(\tfrac{51}{100},\tfrac{18}{25},\tfrac{18}{25})<(\tfrac34,\tfrac34,\tfrac34)=W(y)$ in every coordinate — yet $z_1=\tfrac35>\tfrac12=y_1$. Contestant 1 became weaker while every incumbent's odds fell, because 2 and 3 became much stronger and each incumbent's odds are read off the others. ((SM) survives anyway here: $\Psi_2^{(3)}(z)=\tfrac{171}{500}<\tfrac12$.) So the hypothesis of (SM) genuinely does not pin down the field coordinatewise, and any proof must work with the $W$-data alone.

9. What a violation would have to look like

The premium identity turns (SM) into a sharp statement about who would have to be doing what. Suppose $W_j(z)<W_j(y)$ for all $j$ but $\Psi_k(z)\ge\Psi_k(y)$. Writing $P_j:=W_j-\Psi_k$ for the premium of (7.1),

$$P_j(z)=W_j(z)-\Psi_k(z)\;<\;W_j(y)-\Psi_k(z)\;\le\;W_j(y)-\Psi_k(y)=P_j(y) \qquad\text{for every }j,$$

and, feeding $\sum_j W_j(z)<\sum_j W_j(y)$ into the sum rule (7.2), $k\Pr_z[A=k] < k\Pr_y[A=k] - m(\Psi_k(z)-\Psi_k(y)) \le k\Pr_y[A=k]$. So a counterexample must satisfy both of:

every contestant's pivotality premium $(1-x_j)p_j$ strictly falls, and
the probability that the prizes are exactly filled strictly rises.

That is, the field would have to become simultaneously less pivotal for every single member and more precisely sized. This is the economic content of why the conjecture is believed and why it resists: the obstruction would be a knife-edge coincidence, not a robust mechanism — which is also why the project's falsification searches keep coming back clean.

10. The program form: the shadow set

To prove (SM) one turns it into an optimisation. Fix the reference field $y$ and let

$$K(y)\;:=\;\{x\in[0,1]^m:\ W(x)\le W(y)\ \text{componentwise}\}$$

— the set of fields that are weakly worse than $y$ for every incumbent at once. Because $W$ is coordinatewise increasing, $K(y)$ is a down-set. Then (lem:SMinterior)

$$(\mathrm{SM})\iff \max_{K(y)}\Psi_k \;=\;\Psi_k(y).$$

In words: among all fields that no incumbent prefers to $y$, none gives an entrant better odds than $y$ does. The forward direction is the definition; the converse is a one-line perturbation (shift $x$ down by $\varepsilon\mathbf 1$ to make every inequality strict, apply (SM), let $\varepsilon\downarrow0$).

The interior case is settled by a multiplier computation with an economic reading. At an interior maximiser, KKT gives $\nabla\Psi_k=M\lambda$; the Euler identity for $\Psi_k$ gives $\nabla\Psi_k=Mx^*/(m-k)$; and $M$ is invertible, so

$$\lambda\;=\;\frac{x^*}{m-k}\;>\;0\quad\text{strictly, coordinate by coordinate.}$$

Every shadow price is strictly positive, so by complementary slackness every incumbent's constraint binds: $W(x^*)=W(y)$. There is no such thing as an optimum at which somebody still has room. Injectivity in dimension $m$ then forces $x^*=y$. So (SM) can only fail on the boundary of the cube — and the boundary, it turns out, is where the economics gets interesting.

11. The boundary is a smaller contest

What does a boundary point mean? Set $x_j$ to an endpoint and read off the count.

pinwho $j$ iswhat happens to the countreduced contest
$x_j=1$ a certain loser — surely below, never outranks anyone $j$ contributes $0$ to every above-count, in every event $(k,d)\to(k,d-1)$: delete $j$, keep all $k$ prizes
$x_j=0$ a certain winner — surely above, always takes a slot $j$ contributes $1$ to every above-count, in every event $(k,d)\to(k-1,d-1)$: delete $j$ and one prize

Both lines are one-line computations. If $x_j=1$ then $A=A_{-j}$, so $\Psi_k^{(d)}(x)=\Psi_k^{(d-1)}(x_{-j})$. If $x_j=0$ then $A=A_{-j}+1$, so $\{A\le k-1\}=\{A_{-j}\le k-2\}$ and $\Psi_k^{(d)}(x)=\Psi_{k-1}^{(d-1)}(x_{-j})$. In the project's notation the $x=1$ block is called $O$ and the $x=0$ block $Z$; on a face with $z$ zeros pinned, the surviving level is $\kappa=k-z$.

The invariant. Recall $g=d-k$, the depth of the losing tail. A certain loser consumes a body only, so $g$ drops by one. A certain winner consumes a body and a prize, so $g$ is unchanged: $(d-1)-(k-1)=d-k$. This is the scope invariant $g=n-k$ that keeps recurring in the project's bookkeeping — locks preserve the losing-tail depth, phantoms shorten it. And the closed cases $g=1$ and $g=n-1$ are exactly $k=n-1$ and $k=1$, the two levels where the contest degenerates to "everyone but one wins" and "one wins".

12. Why (SM) is exactly what identification needs

The headline conjecture is that $W$ is injective on $(0,1)^m$: outcome statistics recover the contestants. The local half is a theorem ($\det M\ne0$ everywhere), but local injectivity is not global — the missing ingredient, properness (P), says that the image of the open cube never meets the image of the boundary. Given §11, that is a statement with a plain economic content:

No field of genuine competitors produces the same win-probability vector as a field containing a phantom or a lock. You can never mistake a real contest for a degenerate one.

And (SM) is precisely the lever (lem:facetred). Suppose an interior field $y$ reproduced the win vector of a field $x^*$ containing a phantom $x^*_n=1$. Condition on contestant $n$ under $y$, where he is above with probability $s=1-y_n\in(0,1)$. For $i\ne n$ the reference splits as a mixture of the two reduced contests of §11,

$$W_i(y)=(1-s)\,W_i^{(k,m-1)}(y_{-n})+s\,W_i^{(k-1,m-1)}(y_{-n})\;<\;W_i^{(k,m-1)}(y_{-n}),$$

strictly, because a real rival is worse for $i$ than no rival. Meanwhile $x^*$'s phantom is already absent from the arithmetic, so $W_i(x^*)=W_i^{(k,m-1)}(x^*_{-n})$. Equating the two win vectors therefore gives, on the board of $m-1$,

$$W^{(k,m-1)}(x^*_{-n})\;<\;W^{(k,m-1)}(y_{-n})\ \text{coordinatewise} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \Psi_k(x^*_{-n})=\Psi_k(y_{-n}),$$

the second because the $n$-th coordinate of the win vector is the entrant's value on both sides. (SM) at $(k,m-1)$ says the first display forces $\Psi_k(x^*_{-n})<\Psi_k(y_{-n})$, contradicting the second. Deleting a phantom leaves a field that is strictly worse for everyone yet gives an entrant identical odds — and that is what (SM) forbids.

13. The asymmetry: phantoms are deletable, locks are not

This is the content of sec:odrop, and it explains the shape of everything still open.

Start from §7(b): a phantom has zero premium, so his row of the win vector is the entrant's value, $W_{\hat\jmath}(x)=\Psi_k^{(m)}(x)$ whenever $x_{\hat\jmath}=1$. Now compare the two deletions at a candidate optimum $x$ with reference $y$ interior.

Delete a phantom. For the survivors $i\ne\hat\jmath$, deleting $\hat\jmath$ changes nothing at $x$ (he was inert) but strictly helps at $y$ (he was a real rival there) — quantitatively,

$$W_i^{(k,m-1)}(y_{-\hat\jmath})-W_i^{(k,m)}(y)=(1-y_{\hat\jmath})\Pr\big[A_y([m]\setminus\{i,\hat\jmath\})=k-1\big]>0,$$

which is the premium identity again, now with the pair $\{i,\hat\jmath\}$ set aside. So mere feasibility $W_i(x)\le W_i(y)$ upgrades to a strict inequality on the smaller board, which is exactly the hypothesis of (SM) at $(k,m-1)$. Conclusion (lem:oslack): at every feasible point carrying a phantom, the phantom's own row is strictly slack. No maximality, no stationarity, no regularity is used — this is pencil, and it is why a maximiser cannot carry a phantom (thm:noone): the multiplier sum forces some phantom row to bind, and it cannot.

Delete a lock. Now the reference moves the wrong way. Deleting a certain winner also deletes a prize, so the survivors are compared against a different contest:

$$W_i^{(k,m)}(y)=y_{\hat\jmath}W_i^{(k,m-1)}(y_{-\hat\jmath})+(1-y_{\hat\jmath})W_i^{(k-1,m-1)}(y_{-\hat\jmath}) \;>\;W_i^{(k-1,m-1)}(y_{-\hat\jmath}),$$

so feasibility yields nothing at all and one needs the constraints to bind, which at codimension $\ge2$ is unavailable (the $x=0$ multipliers vanish, leaving the other locks unhandled).

A phantom can be shown the door: removing him leaves the same contest with one fewer body, and everyone left is strictly better off in the reference. A lock cannot: removing him changes the prize count, so the survivors are no longer playing the same game.

This one-sidedness is why the open residual (GAP 3) lives entirely on pure-lock faces, and why the branch with any phantom in it is empty.

15. Where the residual sits

Assembling: the interior of the cube is closed by the multiplier argument of §10; codimension-1 boundary points are closed by the facet template; every point carrying a phantom is closed by §13. What is left is fields that pin two or more contestants at $x=0$ — two or more certain winners — with the rest of the field interior. On such a face the contest reduces to $\kappa=k-z$ prizes among $d=m-z$ genuine contestants, at unchanged losing-tail depth $g$, and the surviving question is whether the entrant's odds can be pushed above the reference while every remaining incumbent stays weakly worse off. That is the open problem, and it is now a question about contests with a known number of guaranteed winners in the room.
